How we check

What GazeVeil looks at, how it rates what it sees, and where it stops. Every GazeVeil report links to this page.

Findings are not legal advice. They are not a verdict on whether a website meets the law, either way.

The steps

What a check does, in order

Only public pages, and only what an ordinary browser does, plus public DNS and certificate lookups.

  1. STEP 1

    Load your public pages

    A real browser opens your site the way a visitor's would, and follows the links on it to your other public pages.

  2. STEP 2

    Read the public records

    The public DNS records and the TLS (https) certificate for your domain, and the security settings your pages send to browsers.

  3. STEP 3

    Open only what reveals something

    It may click a button that only shows something already on the page, such as a contact dialog. A visitor does the same and nothing is sent. It never clicks anything that submits, and never types into a field.

  4. STEP 4

    Count personal data, never keep it

    When it recognises personal data on a page, it records only the type of data and how many it saw. The data itself is thrown away the moment it is recognised.

  5. STEP 5

    Apply written rules

    Each rule ties what was seen to an obligation in the DPDP Act, 2023 or the DPDP Rules, 2025. The rules are plain text files, so the same page always gives the same findings.

  6. STEP 6

    Write the report

    In plain English: what's wrong, why it matters, where we saw it, what's already fine, and what the check couldn't see.

What we look at

Each type of check, in plain words

Cookies, trackers and consent

What your pages load and set as they open, before anyone clicks: analytics, advertising and session-recording tools, and the cookies they set. Also whether personal details end up in web addresses sent to those tools.

The cookie banner

Whether there is one where it is needed, and whether saying no is as easy as saying yes.

Forms and tick boxes

Which forms ask for personal details, whether they send them over a secure connection, whether consent boxes are already ticked, and whether they link to your privacy notice. We read the form. We never fill it in.

The privacy notice

Whether there is one, whether a visitor can find it, and which of the things the Act asks a notice to say it covers, such as who to complain to, a contact for privacy questions, and how people can see, correct and delete their data.

Connection and security settings

Your TLS certificate and https, and the standard security settings browsers use to protect visitors, such as HSTS.

Exposed keys

Secret keys left in the code every visitor's browser downloads. We report that a key is exposed and never use it. Keys that are meant to be public, such as an analytics ID, are not reported.

How findings are rated

Two separate ratings, never mixed

How much an obligation matters and how sure we are that it was missed are different questions, so every finding answers both.

Severity

How much it matters

The legal weight of the obligation. It comes from the Act, not from us.

Confidence

How sure we are

How sure we are that the obligation was missed. It comes from what we saw on the page.

Worth checking. A false alarm is worse than a missed finding. We report something only when the site's owner couldn't fairly say “that's supposed to be there”. When we can't tell from the page alone, we list it as worth checking, say what we saw, and don't count it as a problem.

A finding and a root cause

A finding is one rule that applies, however many pages it appears on. Several findings can share a root cause, the single underlying failure behind them, and are counted once.

Where a language model fits

A language model never decides which part of the Act applies. When we turn one on, it is used only to put a finding into readable words, or to settle the wording of a notice that a plain keyword check couldn't. Every report says whether one was used.

The penalty range

An estimate, not a forecast

Each report shows a possible penalty range. It is built from penalty orders Indian regulators have made in comparable cases. The Data Protection Board has issued no orders under the Act, and penalties can't start before 13 May 2027.

Only the most serious category of finding sets the range. Categories are never added together. The range grows with the number of separate root causes, and never goes above the maximum the Act allows. Only findings we are highly confident about count towards it.

Company size changes the figure, but a website doesn't show it. When size is unknown, the range is made wider and the report says our confidence is low.

The Act asks the Board to weigh things a website can't show, such as how serious a breach was and what the company did about it. Each of those is left neutral. The range shows what regulators have done in comparable cases. It does not say what the Board will do.

What a finding means

What it says, and what it doesn't

A finding means
  • When we looked, a public page showed something that doesn't meet an obligation, or that we couldn't confirm meets it.
  • We can show where we saw it.
  • The same page would give the same finding again.
A finding does not mean
  • That the law has been broken. Only the Board or a court can decide that.
  • That a penalty will follow.
  • That anything we didn't flag is fine. It only means we didn't see a problem on the public side.

Limits

We can only see the public side of a site

A check made without permission doesn't cover these. Every report lists them as not checked, with the reason.

  • Things behind a login, such as whether users can delete their account or download their data.
  • Things inside your company, such as how long you keep data, how fast you answer requests to see or delete it, and how passwords are stored.
  • Files and settings your site doesn't link to. Looking for those means asking your server for addresses a visitor wouldn't ask for, so we do it only with written permission.
  • A few checks we haven't built yet.

With your written permission we can check what a visitor can't see, such as hidden files on your server, your database's access settings and your file storage. The check stays within what the permission allows, and the report logs every action we took and anything we skipped.
